# **Tier: Sovereign** 

## Case: Victoria

Folios: 116r/v, 46r, 48r, 118r

**116r, 116v \- the death announcement:**

***"It is with profound regret that the Political Resident... has directed me to announce"*** 

Davis is *directed* by the political resident, to beard the burden of delivering bad news. The resident isn’t shown to feel anything beyond what a reader wouldn’t witness directly, as Davis is directed to annouce a feeling.Grief here isn't expressed, grief is issued through a chain of command. Davis is a conduit, a messenger, not a mourner. The voice is not personal at all, marking grief as official. This is developed further via the regimented institutional performance of grief, “the flag at the agency should be hoiseted half-mast.” The document does not authorize Davis to appear as a mourner. It authorizes him to perform an institutional act of announcement.

***"Her Most Gracious Majesty Queen Victoria, Queen of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and Empress of India"***

There is no biography here. No age, no cause of death, no family details, nothing that would individuate her as a person. She is entirely coextensive with her titles. This can be further seen as an administrative/bureaucratic procedure because these titles are later repeated but named to King Edward the Seventh, Victoria’s successor.

***"You should communicate the information herein contained to the Trucial Chiefs."*** 

The Chiefs aren't condoled with. They're not addressed. They're informed, downstream, as a formality tacked onto the end of an internal administrative order. Victoria's death produces an occasion for reasserting the chain of command outward, where grief becomes the vehicle for reminding the Gulf rulers of their position in it. 

**118r \- the mourning-order telegram:**

*“Following notification appears… directs as follows”*

The chain of command and provenance are cited, establishing that the telegram is functioning within the bureaucratic machinery.  

*"Deep mourning up to March 6th," "half mourning up to April 17th," crape armbands "up to July 24th."* 

Zero affect vocabulary. Grief here is entirely calendared, it has a start date, a step-down date, an end date, and a dress code. The feeling is fully externalised into schedule and costume; nothing needs to be said, because everything is regulated.

**46r \- the black-edged paper order:**

***"It should be used in all your official correspondence up to the 24th July 1901."*** 

Not correspondence about Victoria, all correspondence. Every document Sharjah produces for the next six months \- a dispute settlement, a labour complaint, anything \- physically carries her mourning on the page, regardless of what it's about, continuing the arbitrariness of the bureaucratic grieving performance. It also makes the power-politics even clearer. The queen’s authority becomes a looming and ghostly figure, marking the edges of the document, where shes is simultaneously both present and absent. The cultural context of the practice may not be decodable by the resident entirely, also showing that colonial power is bound by a socio-lingual barrier that dampens its entry into praxis. They govern, but never fully. 

**In context:**  
Who is allowed to mourn the queen? The throughline across all three is that grief, for Victoria, is never spoken by anyone in their own voice. It's directed, scheduled, and materially imposed. 

# **Tier: Gulf ruler**

## Case: Shaikh Isa 

Folios: 4r, 5r, 10r, 11r, 60r

**4r \-  the death announcement telegram:**

***“Deeply regret to announce the death of Shaikh Isa of Bahrain.”***

The only thing the telegram says. Purely bureaucratic and formal communication method, that is also copied to Baghdad to Kuwait. The subject description is lacking at best when compared to the queen’s; all it says is that this is Shaikh Isa, and where he was from. “Deep regret,” while affective in nature, is used more as administrative short-hand to communicate a “sad” event.  

**5r \- Arabic letter of condolence from the political agent, Bahrain**

***"my mouth is struck silent and my heart is filled with sorrow and grief because of the painful calamity that has befallen Your Highness"***

How much richer and personal the prose suddenly becomes when the correspondence is in Arabic. From the previous "has directed me to announce," Davis is never once the grammatical owner of a feeling. Here, the reverse is true. Loch writes in the first person, as himself, claiming the grief as his own bodily experience ("my heart," "my mouth"). Ontological status and affective voice do not move together here in the way a simple hierarchy would predict. Perhaps the gradation in administrative authority should be accounted for as well. 

***"eloquence of tongue and sweetness of expression are not the true remedy for consoling a wounded heart"***

This sentence disclaims its own eloquence while performing it, at length, in ornamented Arabic prose. 

***"he was an intimate friend \[صديقاً حميماً\] to the British Government"***

This line arrives immediately after "Ruler of Bahrain for a period of sixty-three years," so that sixty-three years of rule is condensed, in the very next clause, into a single relational fact. The sentence does not say what he did for Bahrain. It says what he was to Britain. 

**10r \- Hamad's reply:**

***"I am in receipt of your letter No.1590 dated 11th Shaban 1351 (9th December 1932\) condoling me on the demise of my father"***

The reply opens as a filed response to a numbered letter, not as an outpouring of grief. The bureaucratic frame comes first. Feeling follows only after the reference number is logged.

***"I offer you my sincere thanks from the core of my heart for what you have shown out of sincere love and kindness and for sharing with us in this calamity"***

Hamad answers Loch in Loch's own vocabulary. Calamity, sincere, shared feeling, all repeat 5r almost exactly. The reply is less a spontaneous response than an echo.

***"As Your Excellency has mentioned he ruled Bahrain for a period of 63 years during which he was a great friend to the British Government"***

Hamad does not claim this description as his own. He attributes it directly to Loch. His father's entire reign is remembered through a phrase supplied by the coloniser, and Hamad marks it as borrowed even as he repeats it.

**11r \- Resident to Government of India, and reply:**

**"Regret to announce death of Shaikh Isa of Bahrain morning December 9th. Message of condolence to Shaikh Hamad from His Excellency would be much appreciated."**

The Resident does not offer condolence. He requests permission to have one authorised. Grief for Isa has to be sanctioned from Delhi before it can be issued to Hamad. Compare Victoria's file, where Davis is directed downward to announce. Here the direction reverses. The local officer asks upward, and waits for the message to be handed back to him before it can be given at all.

**"Please convey to Shaikh Hamad sincere condolences of His Excellency the Viceroy."**

Once granted, the condolence is a single stock phrase. No content, no biography, no reference to sixty-three years of rule. This is the bare official channel. Loch's Arabic letter is a separate, unrequested, personal one. Isa's death produces two tracks at once, an authorised formula and a private elaboration, where Victoria's file only ever produces the first.

**In context:**  
The pattern repeats across the file. Telegram requesting authorisation, Arabic letter of condolence, English reply. The condolence is authorised before it is written. This is not evidence that Loch felt more for Isa than the Residency felt for Victoria. It is evidence that Loch, or whoever drafted this on his behalf, was fluent enough in Arabic to code-switch into it for this specific addressee. That fluency is labour Victoria's file never required, because nobody needed to be persuaded to remain loyal to a dead queen's memory. Hamad's continued cooperation needed active management. Grieving language here is a political tool used to manage loyalty.

# **Tier: European subject**

## Case: Father Gabriel Fleming 

Folios: 8r, 11r, 22r, 23r

**8r – the telegram:**

***"Father Gabriel with seven others six of whom were also killed"***

Gabriel is named. The six who died with him are not. They exist only as a subtraction from a total. The telegram answers a family's cable about one man. It has no obligation to individuate the rest, and it doesn't. 

***"Priest unfortunately not available, but I read Prayers for the Dead."***  
Pelly breaks from the report to state something he did himself. No office directs him to feel anything. He states what he did, in his own voice, inside the same message that lists casualties and tonnage.

**11r – Pelly's letter to the sister:**

*"I myself, as a Catholic and a parishioner of Father Gabriel's, off and on for a matter of six years, feel his loss most keenly."*

Pelly names his own relationship to the dead man before he signs off. Grief has a grammatical subject here, and it is Pelly. Loch's Arabic letter to Hamad produced this same first-person register, but only to manage Hamad's loyalty. Fleming secures nothing from Pelly. The grief needs no diplomatic function to justify its presence.

**22r–23r – the burial certificate:**

Luidens asks for "a copy of the burial certificate of Father Gabriel for our cemetery records." Pelly's reply: "No burial certificate appears to have been issued in this case." 

**In context:**  
Fleming gets a Political Agent who grieves in his own voice, unprompted, and himself performs the missing priest's role. Personhood and provenance do not go together here either, as a man mourned by name, in the first person, can still go missing from the record.

# **Tier: Wage labourer**

## Case: Hassan bin Abdulla

Folios: 57r, 58r

**57r – the accident report:**

*"Hassan bin Abdulla, deceased, apparently decided to take a further short-cut by crossing the pitch-pond itself"*

"Apparently" deliberately withdraws the Company as a witness, and thereby, as accountable. His brother and brother-in-law were there; the Company wasn't. The report, though, still assigns him a decision that he himself made, a short-cut, before it assigns him anything else. 

The rescue effort runs to specific numbers. 800 feet from the bank, 1700 feet of greased rope, three hundred 55-gallon drums, an R.A.F. flyover at 2 P.M. No other document in the corpus quantifies effort this precisely for one death. But this, of course, does not imply more care. This is meant to be evidence assembled for a record.

**58r – the liability letter:**

*"While we regret deeply this most unfortunate incident, we wish to record that it was caused by circumstances beyond the Company's control. It would not have happened if the deceased had not trespassed"*

"Deeply" is an intensifier. The lexicon analysis found BAPCO's death-notification letters pairing zero intensifiers with "regret" across five separate cases. This one does. The difference is the work that the regret made to perform. Here it is: the hinge of a legal defence is that it was the individual's sole actions that led to his demise. Regret opens the sentence, blame closes it, and "trespassed" reclassifies Hassan from a dead employee into a boundary violation.

## Case: Nassir bin Awadh

Folio: 27r

*"I claim from the oil Company the rights of my son Abdullah bin Nassir al Awadh, who died while working for the Company as a result of the fall of the pipes."*

Nassir writes as himself, in translation, to claim something. He is the only worker's-family member in the corpus who speaks in the first person about a death. He doesn't request condolence. He requests his son's rights. 

*"I am a poor and an old man, and request you to obtain my rights from the said Oil Company."*

He identifies himself by his poverty and age, not by his relationship to his son, except by naming him once. The letter's heading calls him "Plaintiff." He arrives in the archive already cast in a legal role. His material conditions form the emotional core of the document, the justification for the request, not the grief of losing his son. 

## Case: Amer bin Saad

Folio: 61r

*"We regret to have to inform you of the death of the above-named"*

No intensifier, matching the five other BAPCO notification letters already scored. The same flattening repeats.

*"We hold to credit of deceased's estate the sum of Rs. 22-12-0 for unclaimed wages, and we shall appreciate your instructions regarding the disposal of this amount."*

Amer's death closes on an accounting question. What remains of him in the file is a sum, and what happens to that sum needs instruction. "His brother, Awaz bin Saad", is a data field, "given as," not a person contacted.

**In context:**  
Three worker deaths in this file produce three different kinds of writing. Hassan's death, on Company property, produces a defence. Amer's death, off Company property, produces a routine notice that closes on an unclaimed wage balance. Only Abdullah bin Nassir al Awadh's death produces a first-person account, and it comes from his father, not the Company, arriving already cast as a legal claim. Across all three, affective vocabulary remains aligned with institutional risk and financial closure rather than the worker's ontological status. Grief for a BAPCO worker has no register of its own in this file. It borrows whichever register is closest at hand \- liability, accounting, or petition.

# **Tier: Enslaved**

## Case: Almas

Folios: 7r, 8r, 9r, 9v, 10r

**7r–8r – Almas's testimony:**

***"When I was 8 years old a man of Suwahil kidnapped and sold me to a man of Khazrah of Batinah named Said."***

Almas narrates his own life in the first person, sold three times before manumission, naming each owner and place himself. He supplies the archive with the record. 

***"My manumission was proved in Shara Law Court and Shaikh Hamad heard the witnesses himself and signed the decision of the Shara Court."***

His freedom is established twice before the British government enters the file: once by Khalifah's own act, once by the Shara court. The certificate that the Resident eventually grants doesn't create his freedom. It ratifies a freedom already proved through a legal process the British administration had no part in.

***"I am taking refuge with the High British Government and beg them to be kind enough to favour me with a Government Manumission Certificate so that I may be safe from the threats and molestation of Arabs."***

Almas only asks for papers. He already knows he is free. What he needs is a document British authority will honour against his former master's family, who does not.

**9r–9v – naming Almas:**

***"Presumably Almas has got a nickname — if he doesn't know his father's name — what is to be noted in the certificate."***

The certificate cannot be issued without a name the bureaucracy recognises, and patronymic naming is the only form it knows how to process. Almas has neither.

***"R.A. Sharjah proposes to name Almas's father 'Abdullah' — may this be inserted in the M.C. please?"***

The first solution is to invent a father. Fabricate a genealogy to fill an administrative field.

***"I should think that was risky\!"***

The Resident's own handwritten aside is the only unscripted personal voice a British officer produces in this case, caution about forging a record.

***"R.A. says it best if he was nicknamed as 'Mwas Aniomvaizi' from which tribe has been kidnapped. He belongs to the Aniomvaizi negro tribe of Zanzibar."***

The fabricated father is dropped. In its place, the file settles on his tribal origin. The certificate that finally makes him legible to the state carries no father's name and no name of his own choosing. It carries where he was taken from.

**10r – the certificate:**

***"he should, henceforth, introduce himself as 'Almas An-io-maizi'"***

He is instructed how to say his own name.

**In context:**  
Almas's freedom is proved twice by people who aren't the British government before the British government ever opens the file. What the file adds is a name, and the name it settles on is not a father's, not his own, but the tribe he was taken from. The document that grants him legal personhood renames him after the provenance that personhood was supposed to erase.
